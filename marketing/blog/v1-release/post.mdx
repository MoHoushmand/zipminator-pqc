# Zipminator v1.0: Post-Quantum, On Every Device

*by Mo Houshmand, QDaria*
*2026-04-19*

Q-day is not a hypothetical anymore. The question is not whether a sufficiently capable quantum computer will someday break RSA-2048 and ECDSA P-256, but when. Every encrypted packet recorded today can be decrypted the moment that machine appears. Harvest-now-decrypt-later is already policy for state-level adversaries.

NIST answered that threat in August 2024 when it published FIPS 203, FIPS 204, and FIPS 205: ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA. Today we ship Zipminator v1.0, the first stable release of a post-quantum super-app built end-to-end on those standards.

## What v1.0 Is

Zipminator is encryption infrastructure, not antivirus, not an EDR. It does not scan for malware or sell threat intelligence. It replaces the cryptographic layer under your network traffic, your stored files, and your communications with NIST-standardised post-quantum primitives, and wraps them in a product that works on the phone in your pocket and the laptop on your desk.

## Why A Super-App, Not Nine Apps

The fragmentation tax in security software is real. A user who needs a PQC messenger, a PQC VPN, a PQC password vault, and a PQC email client ends up with four different vendors, four different audit stories, and four different crypto implementations. Every new implementation is a new place for a mistake to live. Zipminator ships all nine pillars on a single Rust crypto core, wrapped in UI surfaces that speak the same FFI bridge. One audit, one KAT suite, one set of fuzz targets, nine user-facing features.

The v1.0 release covers 9 pillars on a single codebase:

- **Quantum Vault**: files encrypted per-object under ML-KEM-768 envelopes, with a DoD 5220.22-M 3-pass self-destruct when you pull the trigger.
- **PQC Messenger**: end-to-end messages with ML-DSA signatures, forward-secret session keys, no classical fallback.
- **Quantum VoIP and Video**: WebRTC calls with PQC-protected signalling and SRTP key exchange.
- **Q-VPN**: a PQ-WireGuard tunnel running today in userspace; kernel module follows in v1.1.
- **10-Level Anonymization**: a ladder from hashing to homomorphic encryption; L1 through L3 are free.
- **Q-AI**: on-device inference plus encrypted remote completion, prompts never leave the device in plaintext.
- **Quantum-Secure Email**: ML-DSA-signed, ML-KEM-enveloped S/MIME replacement with legacy bridges.
- **ZipBrowser**: a Tauri 2.x browser shell that routes through a PQC proxy by default.
- **Q-Mesh**: WiFi Channel-State-Information turned into a physical entropy source, with presence-proof signatures.

## What Is Under the Hood

The crypto core is a from-scratch Rust implementation of ML-KEM-768 and ML-DSA-65. Every secret-dependent path uses `subtle` for constant-time arithmetic; every secret key is wiped via `zeroize` on drop. The core has four `cargo-fuzz` targets and a Known Answer Test suite that runs on every PR.

Python bindings are generated by PyO3 and maturin. The Flutter app reaches the same core via an FFI bridge, which means the bytes that leave your iPhone messenger and the bytes that leave your desktop browser go through exactly one crypto implementation, audited once and reused everywhere.

The single-core design is the part of v1.0 we care most about from a security posture standpoint. Most cross-platform security products reimplement primitives per platform: a JavaScript crypto layer for the web UI, a Swift layer for iOS, a Kotlin layer for Android, a native layer for desktop. Every reimplementation is a new place for a side-channel to leak, for a constant-time invariant to be broken by an eager compiler, or for a key to be kept alive past its zeroize point by a language-level caching behaviour. Zipminator collapses that surface area to one Rust crate under `crates/zipminator-core`, exposed through two thin bridges (PyO3 for Python, `cdylib` FFI for Flutter), and reached from the browser via an HTTPS-plus-ML-KEM tunnel that terminates in a trusted Rust process. A third-party review reads one implementation, not five. A fuzzing campaign fuzzes one implementation, not five. A KAT suite covers one implementation, not five. The cost is the one-time FFI plumbing; the benefit is one audit boundary instead of a matrix.

Entropy for key generation is mixed from four sources: IBM Quantum (Fez, Marrakesh) via qBraid, Rigetti, a pool-backed provider, and the operating-system RNG as fallback. No silent downgrade to OS entropy alone.

## What We Are Not Claiming

v1.0 is not FIPS 140-3 certified. We have not submitted to CMVP yet, and we refuse to use words like "FIPS 140-3 certified" or "FIPS compliant" until we hold a CMVP certificate in hand. What we implement is NIST FIPS 203, FIPS 204, and FIPS 205, verified against the NIST KAT vectors, documented in the code and in the changelog.

Forward-looking throughput numbers you will see in the release notes are labelled Projected or Target. We want the numbers in the v1.1 notes to be measured on release hardware, not inferred from a laptop.

## What Ships Today Versus What Ships Next

Nothing in the v1.0 release depends on hardware that is not on shelves. The Rust core runs on any modern target Rust supports; the Python SDK runs on 3.9 through 3.13; the Flutter app runs on iOS 15+ and Android 10+; the Tauri browser ships DMGs, AppImages, and MSIs. Quantum entropy providers are optional and fall back cleanly to OS RNG with a logged warning, because we do not want a production deployment to hard-fail because a provider like Rigetti or IBM Quantum is having a bad day.

What v1.0 does not ship: a public TestFlight link (Build 47 is still on internal testing), a Google Play open beta, a PQ-WireGuard kernel module, and a CMVP certificate. All four are on the v1.1 plan. None of them block production use of the existing pillars.

## DORA And Regulation-Grade Posture

European operators running under the Digital Operational Resilience Act (Norwegian law since 1 July 2025) have two clauses that matter for crypto: Article 6.4, which requires periodic cryptographic updates based on cryptanalysis developments, and Article 7, which requires full cryptographic key lifecycle management. Those are the quantum-readiness clauses in plain English. A classical-only stack has no auditable plan for 2030 and 2035, when NIST deprecates RSA and ECC. Zipminator v1.0 gives you an auditable one: every key lifecycle event hits a log, every new cryptographic operation runs on ML-KEM or ML-DSA, and the migration path for legacy systems is hybrid by default, not silent downgrade.

What that looks like in practice for an auditor: Art. 7 expects you to answer four questions for each key in circulation. Where was it generated, with what entropy source, who touched it, and when was it destroyed. Zipminator emits a structured audit record at each of those four events, signed with a tenant-scoped ML-DSA audit key, and stores them behind an append-only interface. If your supervisor shows up asking for the lifecycle of a specific session key, you answer with four log lines and a verification command, not a grep across seven systems. Art. 6.4 is the harder clause, because "periodic updates based on cryptanalysis developments" is open-ended by design. Our operational answer is a crypto-parameters manifest checked into the repo at `docs/guides/compliance/`, reviewed on a fixed cadence, and tied to a dependency pin on the PQC dependencies that is upgraded in a single commit the supervisor can diff. When the next round of cryptanalysis lands, the update is a single PR, not an architecture review.

Article 50 matters too, because it sets the penalty floor: up to two percent of global annual turnover for non-compliance. That is the number that makes the legal team read crypto posture documents. For any Norwegian bank or fintech under SB1 or DNB umbrella, the 2030 deprecation clock is not a future risk, it is a current planning horizon. Zipminator's posture is designed to survive that audit without a month of cleanup work before the auditor arrives.

## What Testing Looked Like

The v1.0.0 release runs green on the full matrix: the Rust workspace under `cargo test --workspace`, the Python suite via `pytest tests/`, the Flutter app via `flutter test`, and the Tauri browser shell via `cargo test` inside `browser/src-tauri`. Clippy runs under `-D warnings`. Fuzz corpora for keygen, encapsulate, decapsulate, and round-trip have been running continuously in CI against the Rust core. None of this replaces a third-party crypto review, which is queued behind CMVP submission, but it does mean every commit that lands in v1.0 crossed the same quality gates we will hold v1.1 to.
